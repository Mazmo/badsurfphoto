How to choose a surf school in Portugal

There are plenty of schools along the coast and they are not all the same. These are the questions I would ask, after many days watching lessons from the beach.

Surf guide / Schools

What you can see from the beach

As a photographer, I watch lessons from the outside for hours. I can tell quickly which ones work well: small groups, instructors in the water or right next to the students, a warm-up on land, clear rules before anyone goes in. I also see the opposite, with big groups, little supervision and students in places they should not be.

I do not recommend schools, and BAD SURF PHOTO does not teach. But I can tell you what to look for when deciding, and it has nothing to do with prices or marketing promises.

Licence, registration and insurance

Ask whether the school is registered and licensed for the activity and whether it has insurance, and ask to see the paperwork. Ask too whether the insurance covers students during lessons. A serious school answers without hesitation. Portugal has official registers, and the sports federation also recognises schools and instructors, so it is worth asking who the school is affiliated with.

Instructors should have appropriate training and first-aid knowledge. Do not be shy about asking.

Instructor-to-student ratio

The fewer students per instructor, the more attention and the more safety. Ask for the maximum group size and how many instructors supervise the lesson. As a rule, for beginners, small groups are a sign of quality. Private or two-person lessons cost more but let you correct things faster.

Equipment and the theory and safety briefing

Equipment should be in good condition: boards suited to your level (for beginners, normally soft-tops), wetsuits that fit and sound leashes. A good lesson starts on land, explaining priority rules, how to fall safely, how to read currents and how the sea is behaving that day.

Notice whether the instructor asks about your level, any physical limitations and whether you can swim. That is a good sign.

Flags and lifeguards

Before you get in, look at the beach flag: green means normal conditions, yellow calls for care and red means danger. During the bathing season many beaches have lifeguards and separate zones for swimmers and surfers. A good school respects those zones and chooses not to go in when the sea is not safe.

Reviews and common sense

Read reviews from several sources, but read them critically: look for comments about safety, instructor attention and group size more than atmosphere. Be wary of big promises, such as guaranteeing you will stand up in the first lesson.

FAQ

Quick questions.

How many students per instructor is normal?

There is no magic number, but for beginners fewer is better. Ask for the group maximum and how many instructors are in the water.

Do I need to be able to swim?

Yes, you should be able to swim and feel comfortable in the water. Tell the instructor, who will adapt the lesson and location.
