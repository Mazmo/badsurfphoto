How to photograph surfing from the beach

Photographing surfing is hard for a simple reason: the surfer is far away, the wave keeps moving and the light changes every minute. This guide collects starting points, not fixed rules, from someone who shoots surf from the beach in Portugal every week. If you just want the images and would rather not shoot them yourself, see at the end how to request a session.

Gear

You need a camera with continuous autofocus and fast burst, and a telephoto lens. From the beach, most photographers use lenses between 200 and 400 mm on full-frame cameras; the further out the wave breaks, the more reach you need. A monopod helps on long sessions. Shoot RAW so you can correct exposure and white balance afterwards.

Starting settings

  • Shutter speed: 1/1000 s or faster; 1/1600 to 1/2000 s to freeze spray and manoeuvres.
  • Aperture: f/5.6 to f/8 gives enough depth for the surfer to stay sharp.
  • ISO: automatic with a limit your camera handles well; raise it as the light drops.
  • Focus: continuous (AF-C or AI Servo) with subject tracking, and burst mode.
  • Exposure: water reflects a lot of light and fools the meter. Check the histogram and add exposure when the surfer comes out too dark.

Position and light

Pick a spot on the beach with a view of the peak and the line of the wave, ideally with the sun behind you. Morning and late-afternoon light is lower and warmer. Backlight gives silhouettes and lit-up spray, but needs more care with exposure. Check the tide and wind before you go: they change the shape of the wave and where it breaks.

Composition and timing

Anticipate the wave. Follow the surfer from the paddle and fire a burst on the manoeuvres (bottom turn, top turn, barrel). Leave space in the frame in the direction the surfer is heading. For social media, also think about vertical crops (4:5).

Beach care

Do not stand where surfers enter and leave the water. Protect your gear from sand and salt spray and wipe it afterwards with a slightly damp cloth. Avoid changing lenses in wind and sand.

Common mistakes

  • A shutter speed that is too slow and blurs the manoeuvre.
  • Single-point focus instead of continuous, which loses the moving surfer.
  • Exposing for the bright water, leaving the surfer dark.
  • Shooting only the whole wave without waiting for the manoeuvre.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What shutter speed should I use to photograph surfing?

As a starting point, 1/1000 s or faster. To freeze spray and fast manoeuvres, 1/1600 to 1/2000 s usually works well. Adjust to the light and check the screen.

Which lens is best for photographing surfing from the beach?

A telephoto lens. From the beach, photographers usually use 200 to 400 mm (on full-frame cameras), depending on how far the surfer is.

Can I photograph surfing with a phone?

Yes, especially with optical zoom, good light and burst mode. When the surfer is far away, quality drops quickly; a telephoto lens gives sharper results.

What is the best time of day to photograph surfing?

Early morning and late afternoon, when the light is low and warm. With the sun behind you the colours are richer; backlight gives silhouettes and lit-up spray.
